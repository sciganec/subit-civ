# The Formation and Decline of the Caral-Supe Civilization: A Critical Review of Hypotheses Using the SUBIT Heuristic Framework

*A critical literature synthesis*

2026

---

## Abstract

The Caral-Supe civilization (Norte Chico, north-central coastal Peru, ca. 3500–1800 BCE) is the earliest known cradle of statehood in the Americas and one of only a handful of independent pristine civilizations in world history. Despite more than a quarter-century of systematic research, two key questions remain unresolved in the discipline: (1) which economic subsystem — maritime or agricultural — was the primary driver of monumentality during the formative phase (ca. 3500–2627 BCE), and (2) what was the nature of the decline around 1800 BCE — a sudden collapse or a managed adaptation. This paper is a critical review of the existing primary-source literature on both questions, and it proposes using the formal apparatus of SUBIT — a notational system of states (WHO-WHERE-WHEN), evolution rules, and a dynamic stability classifier Ω — strictly as a heuristic methodological tool for organizing competing hypotheses and formulating discriminating empirical tests. It is emphasized throughout that the SUBIT formalism itself is not a source of new empirical data and does not, on its own, confirm any hypothesis; its function is structural and heuristic.

**Keywords:** Caral-Supe, Norte Chico, Maritime Foundations hypothesis, formal heuristics, Peruvian archaeology, paleodiet, stable isotopes.

---

## 1. Introduction

Ruth Shady Solís's excavations at Caral, begun in 1994, and the subsequent radiocarbon studies by Jonathan Haas, Winifred Creamer, and Alvaro Ruiz (Shady, Haas, & Creamer, 2001; Haas, Creamer, & Ruiz, 2004) pushed back the origins of urban civilization on the Pacific coast of South America by more than a thousand years relative to prior estimates, and established the Norte Chico complex as one of only six independent cradles of civilization in the world — alongside Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, and Mesoamerica.

However, chronological priority did not resolve the debate over the underlying mechanism of emergence. A dispute between proponents of maritime versus agricultural explanations of social complexity has run through Andean archaeology since at least the late 1960s (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022), and the causes of the decline around 1800 BCE remain the subject of active revision to this day, including publications as recent as 2025. The purpose of this paper is not to resolve these debates — that would require new field data — but rather (a) to systematize the existing primary-source literature on both questions in the form of a historiographic review, and (b) to demonstrate how the formal heuristic apparatus of SUBIT can be used to more precisely formulate discriminating empirical tests between competing hypotheses.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 The Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization hypothesis (Moseley, 1975)

Michael Moseley formulated the "Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization" (MFAC) hypothesis, according to which social complexity on the Peruvian coast arose from the exploitation of the extraordinarily rich anchovy (*Engraulis ringens*) and other coastal fisheries of the Humboldt Current, prior to the emergence of irrigation agriculture (Moseley, 1975; as summarized in Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022). The absence of irrigation canals at early coastal settlements, as known at the time the hypothesis was formulated, was interpreted as evidence that agriculture was introduced considerably later — around 1800 BCE — to sustain already-institutionalized social structures.

### 2.2 Dating Caral and the regional chronology (Shady et al., 2001; Haas et al., 2004)

Radiocarbon dating of Caral (Shady, Haas, & Creamer, 2001) showed that this monumental inland site existed contemporaneously with coastal settlements, calling into question a simple "coast-first, then inland" sequence. The broader study by Haas, Creamer, and Ruiz (2004), which encompassed 95 new radiocarbon dates from 13 major sites in the Pativilca and Fortaleza valleys, confirmed the existence of a major cultural complex during the period 3000–1800 BCE and showed that coastal and inland development occurred largely in parallel rather than sequentially.

### 2.3 Zooarchaeological and isotopic studies of 2011: an internal contradiction in the data

A special issue of the *Journal of Island and Coastal Archaeology* (2011) published two studies with opposing conclusions. Creamer, Haas, Jakaitis, and Holguin (2011), comparing marine invertebrates in midden deposits from a coastal (Porvenir) and an inland (Huaricanga) settlement in the Fortaleza Valley, found no substantial difference in the intensity of marine-resource use between them — a result consistent with a model of tight economic integration. By contrast, Coutts, Chu, and Krigbaum (2011), analyzing stable isotopes from human remains at coastal Bandurria compared with inland Caral, reached the opposite conclusion — that the population of Caral relied considerably less on marine resources than the coastal population. This direct contradiction between two contemporaneously published studies illustrates that, as of 2011, the question of the degree of maritime-agricultural integration remained empirically unresolved.

### 2.4 Direct testing of MFAC within a single valley (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022)

A methodological gap in earlier studies (which compared different valleys and different samples) was partially addressed by Pezo-Lanfranco and colleagues, published in the *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*, which analyzed stable isotopes (δ13C, δ15N) from 52 individuals (70 bone and dental samples) directly from Caral and Áspero — the two most important sites within the same Supe Valley. The study found that C3-plant carbohydrates (possibly tubers) dominated the diet at both sites (55–68% of calories at Áspero, over 70% at Caral), while marine protein remained consistently important at coastal Áspero throughout 3300–1800 BCE, but gradually declined at inland Caral over the period 2550–200 BCE. The authors explicitly conclude that their data do not support the MFAC hypothesis in its original formulation, instead pointing to agriculture as dominant from the very outset at both sites, with a gradual weakening of the marine component specifically at the inland settlement.

### 2.5 Geological research on the causes of decline (Sandweiss et al., 2009)

Sandweiss, Solís, Moseley, Keefer, and Ortloff (2009), in a study published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, examined geoarchaeological evidence of environmental change on the Peruvian coast over the period roughly 5,800–3,600 years ago. The study documents a combination of seismic activity and El Niño episodes that produced significant geomorphological changes (siltation of river mouths, landslides) affecting the economic infrastructure of both coastal and inland settlements in the Supe Valley in the period preceding the decline around 1800 BCE.

### 2.6 New data on adaptive migration (Shady, per 2025 reports)

According to reports on Ruth Shady's fieldwork at the sites of Vichama and Peñico, released in 2025, the region experienced a prolonged drought approximately 4,200 years ago, which disrupted agriculture and led to the abandonment of the monumental center of Caral. According to these reports, the population did not disintegrate through conflict or chaotic collapse, but rather migrated along the coast and inland, founding new settlements in which continuity of architectural traditions and symbolic practices can be traced. It should be noted that, at the time of writing this review, this material is available primarily through media and popularization outlets rather than through a peer-reviewed primary publication, which limits the possibility of independent verification within this review.

## 3. Theoretical and Methodological Framework: SUBIT as a Heuristic Tool

### 3.1 The formal apparatus

SUBIT defines a state as a triple of coordinates WHO-WHERE-WHEN, to which the extended version (SUBIT⁺) adds an active evolution rule ρ that belongs to the state itself rather than being externally imposed. The evolution operator F(s, ρ) = (f_ρ(s), g(ρ, s)) simultaneously changes the state and the rule itself. The classifier Ω = {stable, metastable, cyclic, chaotic} determines whether a set of states P is stable with respect to evolution: P is true/stable if and only if F(P) ⊆ P.

### 3.2 Epistemological status of the framework in this study

In this paper, the SUBIT formalism is applied strictly at the hypothesis-construction stage (heuristic scaffolding), not at the verification stage. Classifying a historical transition as "stable," "chaotic," or "metastable" is a qualitative judgment made by the researcher, based on already-existing empirical data, not a computed quantity. Attaching such a label is not, by itself, additional confirmation of a hypothesis; its function is purely organizational — it forces the researcher to formulate a concrete rule ρ (mechanism) and a concrete empirical prediction that distinguishes one hypothesis from another. All factual conclusions in this paper rest exclusively on the sources cited in Section 2 and in the reference list.

## 4. Node 1: The Formative Phase (ca. 3500–2627 BCE)

In SUBIT terms, the competition among three historiographic models of formation can be represented as three alternative rules ρ, each determining which subsystem first reaches a stable state P (F(P) ⊆ P):

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Empirical prediction | Support status per Section 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Maritime priority (MFAC)**<br>Moseley, 1975 | Marine resources produce surplus and sedentism prior to agriculture; coastal sites emerge first and dominate. | Minimal evidence of agriculture in early coastal layers; absent or late irrigation. | **Not supported**: Haas et al. (2013) document early and widespread agriculture; Pezo-Lanfranco et al. (2022) find C3-plant dominance from the outset at both Supe sites. |
| **B. Agricultural/inland priority**<br>Haas, Shady | Irrigation agriculture in inland valleys is the primary source of economic power. | Inland sites larger and older; early evidence of plant cultivation. | **Partially supported**: inland sites are larger in population and scale (Haas & Creamer, 2006), but simultaneity of emergence (Shady et al., 2001) contradicts a strict sequential-priority model. |
| **C. Parallel complementarity**<br>Sandweiss & Moseley (revision) | Maritime and agricultural subsystems develop simultaneously, linked by exchange. | Synchronous emergence; bidirectional evidence of exchange. | **Partially supported and partially contradicted**: Creamer et al. (2011) support tight integration; Coutts et al. (2011) contradict it; Pezo-Lanfranco et al. (2022) document a weakening of the marine component at Caral over time, which contradicts a stable, symmetric complementarity. |

A cumulative analysis of the sources reviewed in 2.1–2.4 indicates that Hypothesis A is, at present, the least well supported by the available primary data. The dispute between B and C is not resolved: the most precise study available (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022), which compares the same river basin at the same time, documents not a stable symmetric complementarity but an asymmetric, time-varying balance — agriculture dominates at both sites from the outset, while the marine component remains consistently significant on the coast and gradually weakens inland. This is best described not as the victory of one model, but as a dynamic gradient that itself changes over time — an observation important for the discussion in Section 6.

## 5. Node 2: The Decline Phase (ca. 1800 BCE)

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Empirical prediction | Support status per Section 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Geological-climatic shock**<br>Sandweiss et al., 2009 | A combination of seismic activity and intensified El Niño episodes causes geomorphological disruption (siltation, landslides). | Stratigraphic evidence of abrupt geomorphological change preceding or coinciding with site abandonment. | **Supported** at the geoarchaeological level (Sandweiss et al., 2009); however, the study describes a process extended over time (5,800–3,600 years ago), not a single catastrophic event. |
| **B. Gradual irrigation decay** | Cyclical El Niño episodes progressively undermine canal irrigation; inland sites weaken before coastal ones. | Asynchronous, geographically graded site abandonment. | **Neither supported nor refuted** in the reviewed sources: no direct comparative dating of inland versus coastal site abandonment in the Supe Valley was found in this review — an open empirical gap. |
| **C. Prolonged drought with adaptive migration**<br>Shady, 2025 reports | Drought (possibly linked to the broader global 4.2-kyr event) disrupts agriculture; population migrates while preserving cultural practices. | Continuity of architectural and symbolic markers in new settlements (Vichama, Peñico) without a break in material culture. | **Tentatively supported** by available reports, but the source is not, at the time of this review, a peer-reviewed publication — independent confirmation in an academic venue is needed. |

The data reviewed in 2.5–2.6 are not mutually exclusive: a geologically documented process (Sandweiss et al., 2009) may have created conditions of vulnerability, against which a later prolonged drought (per 2025 reports) became the proximate trigger for the abandonment of Caral. In SUBIT terms, this corresponds not to a single stable → chaotic transition, but to a gradual metastable weakening followed by a metaevolution of the rule (the g-function) — a replacement of the rule "maintain the monumental center in place" with the rule "migrate while preserving cultural invariants." This classification is an interpretive proposal of this paper, not an established fact, and requires verification through direct comparative dating of the geological and drought horizons in future fieldwork.

## 6. Discussion

Compared to a purely narrative account, the heuristic framework applied in this paper yielded three concrete methodological results. First, it forced the decomposition of the vague category "cause of decline" into distinct, empirically differentiable mechanisms, each with its own stratigraphic or isotopic signature. Second, it revealed that the primary-source literature on the formative phase is itself internally contradictory (Creamer et al., 2011 versus Coutts et al., 2011), and that even the most precise available study (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022) does not confirm any of the three hypotheses in pure form, but instead describes a time-varying gradient. Third, it provided a vocabulary for describing the decline not as a binary "collapse / no collapse," but as a category of metastable rule-reconfiguration — which precisely matches the documented pattern of "abandoned the city, preserved the culture" (per reports on Vichama and Peñico).

At the same time, the limits of this approach must be stressed. None of the Ω-classifications presented here is a computed quantity; they are qualitative judgments by the researcher, fitted post hoc to already-known facts, and the mere fact that a label "fits well" is not additional empirical confirmation. This paper is a secondary literature synthesis: no new stratigraphic, isotopic, or radiocarbon analysis was conducted within it. Where the sources used in the review contradict one another (2.3), or where a key source is not a peer-reviewed publication (2.6), this reflects a genuine, still-unresolved state of the discipline, rather than evidence in favor of any hypothesis formulated here.

---

## 7. Conclusions

The formation of Caral-Supe is most accurately described not as the victory of the maritime subsystem over the agricultural one, or vice versa, but as an asymmetric, time-varying economic gradient, as documented by the most precise direct study available (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022); the pure Maritime Foundations hypothesis (Moseley, 1975) is, at present, the least well supported. The decline around 1800 BCE most likely combines a documented geological-climatic process (Sandweiss et al., 2009) with a later prolonged drought, after which the population did not disintegrate but migrated while preserving cultural continuity — though this latter source requires confirmation in a peer-reviewed publication. The SUBIT heuristic framework proved useful as a tool for formulating discriminating tests and for categorizing types of historical transition, but not as a source of new empirical claims: all factual conclusions in this paper rest exclusively on the primary sources listed below.

## 8. Glossary of SUBIT Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **State (s)** | A triple of coordinates WHO-WHERE-WHEN describing the elementary unit of system description. |
| **Extended state (s⁺)** | A pair (state, rule) = (s, ρ); the evolution rule is part of the state itself, not an external parameter. |
| **Evolution rule (ρ)** | A function determining how a state changes at the next step; in this paper, a concrete historical mechanism (e.g., maritime priority, drought). |
| **Evolution operator (F)** | F(s, ρ) = (f_ρ(s), g(ρ, s)): simultaneously changes the state and the rule itself (metaevolution). |
| **Ω-classifier** | A four-valued classification of the stability of a set of states: stable, metastable, cyclic, chaotic. |
| **Stable** | F(P) ⊆ P: a set of states reproduces itself under evolution (e.g., the durable replication of a monumental model). |
| **Metastable** | F(P) ⊆ P, but P ⊄ F(P): partial, not fully reversible stability; in this paper, a gradual weakening preceding a transition to a new rule. |
| **Cyclic** | ∃k > 0: Fᵏ(P) = P: orbital repetition without final fixation (e.g., replication of a single settlement model across multiple sites). |
| **Chaotic** | Absence of a stable or cyclic set; used in this paper as a contrast category to "metastable reconfiguration," not as a confirmed description of Caral's decline. |
| **Discriminating test** | An empirical observation that yields different, mutually exclusive predictions for competing hypotheses — a methodological, not a formal, element of SUBIT, added in this paper to connect the heuristic to testable data. |
