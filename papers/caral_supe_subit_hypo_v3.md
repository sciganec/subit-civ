# The Formation and Decline of the Caral-Supe Civilization: A Critical Review of Hypotheses Using the SUBIT Heuristic Framework

*A critical literature synthesis (revised version)*

2026

---

## Abstract

The Caral-Supe civilization (Norte Chico, north-central coastal Peru, ca. 3500 – ca. 1800 BCE) is the earliest known cradle of statehood in the Americas. Despite more than a quarter-century of systematic research, two key questions remain unresolved in the discipline: (1) which economic subsystem — maritime or agricultural — was the primary driver of monumentality during the early phase (ca. 3500 – ca. 2600 BCE, a conventional boundary based on median radiocarbon estimates from Shady, Haas, & Creamer, 2001; Haas, Creamer, & Ruiz, 2004), and (2) what was the nature of the decline around 1800 BCE. This paper is a critical review of the existing primary-source literature on both questions and proposes using the formal apparatus of SUBIT — a notational system of states (WHO-WHERE-WHEN), evolution rules, and a dynamic stability classifier Ω — strictly as a heuristic methodological tool for organizing competing hypotheses and formulating discriminating empirical tests. This revised version corrects the bibliographic apparatus (journal renaming, DOIs), tempers an overly categorical reading of the geological decline data, adds a newly identified peer-reviewed 2025 source on Vichama, and expands the discriminating tests for the formative phase.

**Keywords:** Caral-Supe, Norte Chico, Maritime Foundations hypothesis, formal heuristics, Peruvian archaeology, paleodiet, stable isotopes.

---

## 1. Introduction

Ruth Shady Solís's excavations at Caral, begun in 1994, and the subsequent radiocarbon studies by Jonathan Haas, Winifred Creamer, and Alvaro Ruiz (Shady, Haas, & Creamer, 2001; Haas, Creamer, & Ruiz, 2004) pushed back the origins of urban civilization on the Pacific coast of South America by more than a thousand years relative to prior estimates, and established the Norte Chico complex as one of only six independent cradles of civilization in the world.

However, chronological priority did not resolve the debate over the underlying mechanism of emergence: the dispute between proponents of maritime versus agricultural explanations of social complexity has run since the late 1960s (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022), and the nature of the decline around 1800 BCE remains the subject of active revision, including a peer-reviewed 2025 publication. The purpose of this paper is to systematize the existing primary-source literature on both questions and to demonstrate how the formal heuristic apparatus of SUBIT can be used to more precisely formulate discriminating empirical tests between competing hypotheses, without substituting for empirical verification.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 The Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization hypothesis (Moseley, 1975)

Michael Moseley formulated the "Maritime Foundations of Andean Civilization" (MFAC) hypothesis, according to which social complexity on the Peruvian coast arose from the exploitation of the extraordinarily rich anchovy (*Engraulis ringens*) fisheries of the Humboldt Current, prior to the emergence of irrigation agriculture (Moseley, 1975; as summarized in Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022).

### 2.2 Dating Caral and the regional chronology (Shady et al., 2001; Haas et al., 2004)

Dating of Caral (Shady, Haas, & Creamer, 2001, *Science* 292, 723–726, doi:10.1126/science.1059519) showed that this monumental inland site existed contemporaneously with coastal settlements. The broader study by Haas, Creamer, and Ruiz (2004, *Nature* 432, 1020–1023, doi:10.1038/nature03146), encompassing 95 radiocarbon dates from 13 sites in the Pativilca and Fortaleza valleys, confirmed the existence of a major cultural complex during 3000–1800 BCE and showed that coastal and inland development occurred largely in parallel.

### 2.3 Zooarchaeological and isotopic studies of 2011: an internal contradiction in the data

A special issue of the *Journal of Island and Coastal Archaeology* (2011) published two studies with opposing conclusions. Creamer, Haas, Jakaitis, and Holguin (2011, doi:10.1080/15564894.2011.582071), comparing marine invertebrates in midden deposits from a coastal (Porvenir) and an inland (Huaricanga) settlement, found no substantial difference in marine-resource use intensity between them. By contrast, Coutts, Chu, and Krigbaum (2011, doi:10.1080/15564894.2011.586086), analyzing stable isotopes from human remains at coastal Bandurria compared with inland Caral, reached the opposite conclusion. This direct contradiction between two contemporaneously published studies illustrates that, as of 2011, the degree of maritime-agricultural integration remained empirically unresolved.

### 2.4 Direct testing of MFAC within a single valley (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022)

A methodological gap in earlier studies was partially addressed by Pezo-Lanfranco and colleagues, published in the *American Journal of Biological Anthropology* (the journal was renamed from the *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* in 2022; Pezo-Lanfranco, Machacuay, Novoa, Peralta, Mayer, Eggers, & Shady, 2022, *Am. J. Biol. Anthropol.* 177(3), 402–424, doi:10.1002/ajpa.24445). The study analyzed stable isotopes (δ13C, δ15N) from 52 individuals (70 samples) directly from Caral and Áspero — the two most important sites in the Supe Valley — and found that C3-plant carbohydrates dominated the diet at both sites (55–68% of calories at Áspero, over 70% at Caral), while marine protein remained consistently important at coastal Áspero but gradually declined at inland Caral over time. The authors explicitly conclude that their data do not support the MFAC hypothesis in its original formulation.

### 2.5 Geological research on the causes of decline (Sandweiss et al., 2009)

Sandweiss, Solís, Moseley, Keefer, and Ortloff (2009, *PNAS* 106(5), 1359–1363, doi:10.1073/pnas.0812645106) examined environmental change on the Peruvian coast over a broader period of ≈5,800–3,600 cal BP, during which the largest monumental settlements in the Western Hemisphere flourished, based on intensive fishing and irrigated orchards without ceramics or loom weaving. Within this broader window, the authors document a specific cycle of natural disasters — earthquakes, El Niño flooding, beach-ridge formation, and dune incursion — dated to approximately 3,800 cal BP (≈1850 BCE), after which this economic model was abruptly abandoned and gradually replaced by societies relying more on food crops, pottery, and weaving. This date (≈1850 BCE) sits very close to the conventional decline marker (~1800 BCE), making it not merely a distant precondition of vulnerability but a plausible direct trigger of the transition itself.

### 2.6 New data on the post-Caral period: Vichama (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2025)

In contrast to 2025 media reports (which mention a drought ~4,200 years ago and the site of Peñico without detailed peer-reviewed confirmation), a peer-reviewed article by the same research group appeared in July 2025 in *Frontiers in Environmental Archaeology* (Pezo-Lanfranco, Crispin, Prado-Barragán, Abad, Machacuay, Yseki, Gorriti, Miranda, Apolín, DiMuro, Novoa, Colonese, & Shady, 2025, *Front. Environ. Archaeol.* 4:1611071, doi:10.3389/fearc.2025.1611071). The study analyzed stable isotopes from 38 individuals at Vichama (Huaura Valley) across two periods — Early Formative-1 (EF-1, 1800–1500 BCE) and the Late Intermediate Period (1000–1300 CE). The authors explicitly state that Vichama likely represented the rise of a new sociopolitical center in the region following the decline of the Caral society, and document, in the EF-1 phase (which begins exactly at the conventional Caral-decline marker, ~1800 BCE), a farming-fishing economy dominated by C3 plants — that is, an economic model continuous with the earlier Formative tradition rather than a break from it.

An important caveat: this peer-reviewed article does not directly test the "drought caused migration" narrative and does not mention the Peñico site; its subject is dietary reconstruction and its relationship to shifts in marine productivity (El Niño/La Niña cycles), not the causes of Caral's abandonment per se. The appropriately scoped conclusion is therefore narrower than the media framing: the peer-reviewed literature currently confirms the emergence of a new coastal center (Vichama) immediately after Caral's decline, with continuity of the economic tradition, but does not independently confirm the specific "megadrought → organized migration" causal mechanism, which remains at the level of media reports pending a study specifically devoted to the causes of Caral's abandonment.

## 3. Theoretical and Methodological Framework: SUBIT as a Heuristic Tool

### 3.1 The formal apparatus and a worked example

SUBIT defines a state as a triple of coordinates WHO-WHERE-WHEN, to which the extended version (SUBIT⁺) adds an active evolution rule ρ belonging to the state itself. The evolution operator F(s, ρ) = (f_ρ(s), g(ρ, s)) simultaneously changes the state and the rule. The classifier Ω = {stable, metastable, cyclic, chaotic} determines whether a set of states P is stable: P is stable if and only if F(P) ⊆ P.

**Worked example.** Let s = (WHO: the Supe Valley community, WHERE: Caral, WHEN: ~2600 BCE), and ρ = "mobilize a surplus of C3 tubers to build a monumental platform." Then F(s, ρ) yields a new architectural phase together with the reproduction (reinforcement) of that same rule ρ in the next cycle. If this reproduction persists — that is, the set of states P generated by this rule satisfies F(P) ⊆ P — the classification Ω = stable correctly describes a self-reproducing system. If, at the ~1800 BCE marker, the rule ρ is replaced by ρ' = "migrate along the coast, founding a new center (Vichama), while preserving the farming-fishing model," this is a metaevolution (the g-function), not a simple failure of F(P) ⊆ P: the transition is more accurately described as metastable rather than chaotic, since the economic and symbolic structure is partially inherited by the new rule.

### 3.2 Epistemological status of the framework in this study

In this paper, the SUBIT formalism is applied strictly at the hypothesis-construction stage, not at the verification stage. Assigning an Ω-label is not, by itself, empirical confirmation; its function is to force the formulation of a concrete rule ρ and a concrete empirical prediction distinguishing one hypothesis from another. All factual conclusions in this paper rest exclusively on the sources cited in Section 2 and in the reference list.

## 4. Node 1: The Formative Phase (ca. 3500 – ca. 2600 BCE)

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Empirical prediction | Support status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Maritime priority (MFAC)**<br>Moseley, 1975 | Marine resources produce surplus and sedentism prior to agriculture; coastal sites emerge first and dominate. | Minimal evidence of agriculture in early coastal layers. | **Not supported**: Haas et al. (2013, doi:10.1073/pnas.1219425110) document early and widespread agriculture; Pezo-Lanfranco et al. (2022) find C3-plant dominance from the outset at both Supe sites. |
| **B. Agricultural/inland priority**<br>Haas, Shady | Irrigation agriculture in inland valleys is the primary source of economic power. | Inland sites larger and older; the earliest irrigation canals predate coastal monumentality. | **Partially supported** on scale (Haas & Creamer, 2006, doi:10.1086/506281); the direct test (age of the earliest Supe canals) is an open empirical gap — no published data on Supe Valley canals predating 3000 BCE were found in the reviewed sources. |
| **C. Parallel complementarity**<br>Sandweiss & Moseley (revision) | Maritime and agricultural subsystems develop simultaneously, linked by exchange. | Synchronous emergence; bidirectional evidence of exchange. | **Partially supported and partially contradicted**: Creamer et al. (2011) support tight integration; Coutts et al. (2011) contradict it; Pezo-Lanfranco et al. (2022) document a weakening of the marine component at Caral over time, contradicting a stable, symmetric model. |

A cumulative analysis of the sources in 2.1–2.4 indicates that Hypothesis A is, at present, the least well supported. The dispute between B and C is not resolved: the most precise study (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022) documents an asymmetric, time-varying balance rather than a stable symmetric complementarity — agriculture dominates from the outset, while the marine component remains consistently significant on the coast and gradually weakens inland. The key discriminating test between B and C — the relative age of the earliest irrigation canals in the Supe Valley compared to the earliest coastal monumentality — remains untested at present due to a lack of published data specific to this valley.

## 5. Node 2: The Decline Phase (ca. 1800 BCE)

| Hypothesis | Mechanism | Empirical prediction | Support status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Geological-climatic shock**<br>Sandweiss et al., 2009 | A cycle of natural disasters (earthquakes, El Niño flooding) dated to ≈3,800 cal BP (≈1850 BCE) disrupts economic infrastructure. | Stratigraphic evidence of abrupt geomorphological change around ≈1850 BCE. | **Supported** at the geoarchaeological level (Sandweiss et al., 2009) as a specifically dated disaster cycle immediately preceding the decline — not merely a distant precondition, but a plausible direct trigger. |
| **B. Gradual irrigation decay** | Cyclical El Niño episodes progressively undermine canal irrigation; inland sites weaken before coastal ones. | Asynchronous, geographically graded site abandonment. | **Neither supported nor refuted**: no direct comparative dating of coastal versus inland site abandonment in the Supe Valley was found in the reviewed sources. |
| **C. Drought with adaptive migration and continuity** | Climatic stress disrupts agriculture; the population founds new centers (Vichama) while preserving the economic model. | A new center emerges shortly after Caral's abandonment, with economic/cultural continuity rather than a break. | **Partially supported** by a peer-reviewed source: Pezo-Lanfranco et al. (2025) document the rise of Vichama exactly at the ~1800 BCE marker, with a continuous farming-fishing model. The specific "megadrought" mechanism and the Peñico site remain at the level of media reports, not independently peer-reviewed. |

The data in 2.5–2.6 combine into a coherent scenario: a documented disaster cycle around 1850 BCE (Sandweiss et al., 2009) may have been the proximate trigger for Caral's abandonment, after which the population founded a new coastal center, Vichama, which arose exactly at the ~1800 BCE marker with a continuous economic model (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2025). In SUBIT terms, this corresponds to a metastable rule-reconfiguration (the g-function), not a chaotic disintegration: the system did not lose its invariant structure entirely but relocated it. Hypothesis B (gradual irrigation decay) remains empirically neither confirmed nor refuted due to a lack of direct comparative data for the Supe Valley.

## 6. Discussion

Compared to a purely narrative account, the applied framework yielded three concrete methodological results. First, it decomposed the vague category "cause of decline" into distinct mechanisms, each with its own stratigraphic or isotopic signature. Second, it revealed that the primary-source literature on the formative phase is internally contradictory (Creamer et al., 2011 versus Coutts et al., 2011), and that even the most precise study (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022) describes a time-varying gradient rather than the victory of one model. Third, it provided a vocabulary for describing the decline not as a binary "collapse/no collapse," but as a metastable rule-reconfiguration — a category that, following the incorporation of the 2025 peer-reviewed source on Vichama, now has direct empirical support for the economic-continuity component.

The limits of this approach must be stressed. None of the Ω-classifications is a computed quantity; these are qualitative judgments by the researcher based on already-existing data. This paper is a secondary literature synthesis: no new stratigraphic, isotopic, or radiocarbon analysis was conducted within it. The B-versus-C discriminating test for the formative phase (age of the earliest Supe canals) remains an open empirical gap rather than a refuted hypothesis; likewise, the specific "megadrought → organized migration to Peñico" mechanism for the decline phase is currently confirmed only at the level of media reports, not in a peer-reviewed study specifically devoted to the causes of Caral's abandonment.

---

## 7. Conclusions

The formation of Caral-Supe is most accurately described as an asymmetric, time-varying economic gradient (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2022), rather than the victory of the maritime or agricultural subsystem; the pure Maritime Foundations hypothesis (Moseley, 1975) is the least well supported. The decline around 1800 BCE plausibly combines a documented cycle of geological-climatic disasters ≈1850 BCE (Sandweiss et al., 2009) with the subsequent founding of a new coastal center, Vichama, with a continuous economic model (Pezo-Lanfranco et al., 2025) — a scenario more consistent with metastable rule-reconfiguration than with chaotic collapse. The specific "megadrought" causal mechanism and the role of the Peñico site require separate peer-reviewed confirmation. The SUBIT heuristic framework proved useful for formulating discriminating tests and categorizing types of historical transition, but not as a source of new empirical claims.

## 8. Glossary of SUBIT Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **State (s)** | A triple of coordinates WHO-WHERE-WHEN describing the elementary unit of system description. |
| **Extended state (s⁺)** | A pair (state, rule) = (s, ρ); the evolution rule is part of the state itself, not an external parameter. |
| **Evolution rule (ρ)** | A function determining how a state changes at the next step; in this paper, a concrete historical mechanism. |
| **Evolution operator (F)** | F(s, ρ) = (f_ρ(s), g(ρ, s)): simultaneously changes the state and the rule itself (metaevolution). |
| **Ω-classifier** | A four-valued classification of stability: stable, metastable, cyclic, chaotic. |
| **Stable** | F(P) ⊆ P: a set of states reproduces itself under evolution. |
| **Metastable** | F(P) ⊆ P, but P ⊄ F(P): partial stability; in this paper, a rule-reconfiguration with partial inheritance of structure (e.g., relocation of a settlement). |
| **Cyclic** | ∃k > 0: Fᵏ(P) = P: orbital repetition. Important clarification: in this study, cyclic does not mean seasonality or calendrical periodicity, but the replication of a single settlement model across space (multiple sites built to the same "recipe"). |
| **Chaotic** | Absence of a stable or cyclic set; a contrast category to "metastable reconfiguration," not a confirmed description of Caral's decline. |
| **Discriminating test** | An empirical observation with different, mutually exclusive predictions for competing hypotheses — a methodological element added in this paper to connect the heuristic to testable data. |
